Part 1 · Syllabus Theory (Simplified & Clear)

2.1 Why Signal Conditioning?

💡 In Simple Terms: Raw sensor signals are like raw crude oil: tiny (microvolts), full of noise, high-impedance, and distorted. You cannot feed them directly to a controller or display. Signal conditioning is the refinery: it amplifies, cleans (filters), linearises, isolates, and converts the raw signal into a clean, standard industrial signal (like $4\text{ to }20\text{ mA}$ or $0\text{ to }10\text{ V}$).

Core Operations: (1) Amplification (boosts millivolt thermocouple/strain signals), (2) Filtering (strips $50\text{ Hz}$ mains hum and high-frequency noise), (3) Isolation (breaks dangerous ground loops), (4) Linearisation (straightens non-linear sensor curves), (5) Signal Conversion (e.g., Charge-to-Voltage, Resistance-to-Voltage, Current-to-Pressure).

2.2 Differential Amplifier

💡 In Simple Terms: Like noise-cancelling headphones! In a factory, electromagnetic noise hits both sensor wires equally (common-mode noise). A differential amplifier subtracts one wire from the other. The identical noise on both wires is subtracted away to zero, while the true sensor signal between the two wires is boosted! 
              R1                 R2
 V1 o--------/\/\/---+---------/\/\/---+
                     |   |\            |
                     +---| - \         |
                         |     >-------+--------o Output Vo
                     +---| + /
              R1     |   |/
 V2 o--------/\/\/---+---------/\/\/---+
                                 R2    |
                                      GND

Circuit Derivation

Using superposition for matched resistor pairs ($R_1$ and $R_2$):

  1. With $V_2 = 0$: Inverting configuration $\implies V_{o1} = -\dfrac{R_2}{R_1} V_1$.
  2. With $V_1 = 0$: Non-inverting configuration where voltage at $(+)$ terminal is $V_+ = V_2\,\dfrac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$. $$ V_{o2} = \left(1 + \frac{R_2}{R_1}\right) V_+ = \left(\frac{R_1 + R_2}{R_1}\right) \left(\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\right) V_2 = \frac{R_2}{R_1} V_2 $$
  3. Summing both responses gives the output: $$ V_o = V_{o1} + V_{o2} = \frac{R_2}{R_1}\,(V_2 - V_1) $$

Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR): Measures the ability to reject noise: $\text{CMRR} = \left|\dfrac{A_d}{A_{cm}}\right|$.

Limitations of Basic Differential Amp: (1) Input impedance is relatively low (equal to $R_1$), which loads sensitive sensors; (2) To change gain, two resistors must be matched and changed simultaneously.

2.3 Instrumentation Amplifier (IA)

💡 In Simple Terms: The ultimate precision amplifier. It takes two high-impedance buffer amplifiers and places them in front of a differential amplifier. A single resistor $R_G$ adjusts the gain, input impedance is practically infinite, and CMRR reaches $100\text{–}120\text{ dB}$! 
 V1 o--------+\ A1                        R2        R3
             |  >--+--- R1 ---+------/\/\/----+
          +--+/    |          |               |   |\ A3
          |        Rg(gain)   +--- Vo1        +---| - \
          |        |                              |     >---o Vo
          |        |          +--- Vo2 -------+---| + /
          +--+\    |          |          R2   |   |/
             |  >--+--- R1 ---+               /\/\/ R3 -- GND
 V2 o--------+/ A2

Gain Derivation

Stage 1: Buffer / Pre-Amplifier Stage (A1 & A2):

  1. By op-amp virtual short, the inverting terminals are at voltages $V_1$ and $V_2$.
  2. The voltage drop across gain resistor $R_G$ is strictly $(V_1 - V_2)$.
  3. Current flowing through $R_G$ is: $I_G = \dfrac{V_1 - V_2}{R_G}$.
  4. Since op-amp inputs draw zero current, the exact same current $I_G$ flows through both upper and lower $R_1$ resistors: $$ V_{o1} = V_1 + I_G R_1, \qquad V_{o2} = V_2 - I_G R_1 $$
  5. Subtracting $V_{o2}$ from $V_{o1}$: $$ \begin{aligned} V_{o1} - V_{o2} &= (V_1 - V_2) + 2 I_G R_1 \\ &= (V_1 - V_2)\left(1 + \frac{2R_1}{R_G}\right) \end{aligned} $$

Stage 2: Difference Amplifier Stage (A3):

Stage 3 amplifies the difference by factor $\dfrac{R_3}{R_2}$:

$$ V_o = \frac{R_3}{R_2}\,(V_{o2} - V_{o1}) = \boxed{\,\frac{R_3}{R_2}\left(1 + \frac{2R_1}{R_G}\right)(V_2 - V_1)\,} $$
🎯 Key Advantage: For common-mode signals ($V_1 = V_2$), no current flows through $R_G$ ($I_G = 0$). Thus, common-mode gain is strictly unity in stage 1, while differential signals are boosted by $(1 + 2R_1/R_G)$. This provides phenomenal noise immunity!

2.4 Precision Rectifiers

💡 In Simple Terms: An ordinary silicon diode is "blind" to any signal below $0.7\text{ V}$ (its forward barrier potential $V_\gamma$). If a sensor produces a $20\text{ mV}$ AC signal, a standard diode produces zero! A precision rectifier puts the diode inside the op-amp's feedback loop. The huge op-amp gain ($A_{ol} \approx 10^5$) crushes the threshold to $0.7\text{ V} / 10^5 = 7\,\mu\text{V}$, allowing microvolt AC signals to be rectified perfectly!

1. Half-Wave Precision Rectifier

                        Diode D
 Vin o-------+\           |\|
             |  >---------| >|---+--------o Output Vo
        +----|-/          |/|    |
        |    |                   |       RL
        +----+-------------------+------/\/\/--- GND (Feedback taken AFTER diode)
  • Positive Half-Cycle ($V_{in} > 0$): Op-amp output goes positive $\implies$ Diode conducts $\implies$ Feedback loop closes $\implies$ Virtual short forces $V_o = V_{in}$.
  • Negative Half-Cycle ($V_{in} < 0$): Op-amp output swings negative $\implies$ Diode is reverse-biased (open) $\implies$ No current in load $R_L \implies V_o = 0$.
$$ V_o = \begin{cases} V_{in}, & V_{in} > 0 \\ 0, & V_{in} \le 0 \end{cases} $$

2. Full-Wave Precision Rectifier (Absolute Value Circuit)

              Stage 1: Inverting Half-Wave Rectifier            Stage 2: Summing Inverter
                           D1
    Vin o---+---/\/\/---+---|<-+                     Vin o-----/\/\/----+
            |     R     |  [A1]|                          R      |  |\ A2
            |           +---|>-+---+ V1              V1 o-----/\/\/----+| - \
            |              D2                                R/2        |     >---o Vo = |Vin|
            +-----------------------------------------------------------| + /
                                                                        |/  (Rf = R)

Stage 1 outputs $V_1 = -V_{in}$ when $V_{in} > 0$ and $V_1 = 0$ when $V_{in} < 0$. Stage 2 sums $V_{in}$ (weight 1) and $V_1$ (weight 2):

$$ V_o = -\left[\frac{R}{R} V_{in} + \frac{R}{R/2} V_1\right] = -(V_{in} + 2 V_1) $$
  • When $V_{in} > 0$: $V_1 = -V_{in} \implies V_o = -(V_{in} - 2V_{in}) = +V_{in}$.
  • When $V_{in} < 0$: $V_1 = 0 \implies V_o = -(V_{in} + 0) = -V_{in} = |V_{in}|$.

Thus, $V_o = |V_{in}|$ for all inputs. The output is an accurate full-wave rectified signal without diode drop losses.

2.5 Logarithmic Amplifier

💡 In Simple Terms: In sound, light, or earthquake measurement, signals vary over a gigantic range (from $1\text{ mV}$ to $10\text{ V}$—a 10,000-to-1 ratio!). A standard linear amplifier would clip or saturate. A log amplifier compresses this huge range onto a compact scale using the natural exponential physics of a bipolar transistor. 
               +------- Transistor Q (Collector to (-), Base to GND, Emitter to Vo) ---+
               |                                                                       |
 Vin o---/\/\/---+--------|\                                                            |
          R      |        | - \                                                         |
                 +--------|     >-------------------------------------------------------+--o Output Vo
                          | + /
                   GND ---|/

Step-by-Step Derivation

  1. The inverting terminal is at virtual ground ($0\text{ V}$). Input current is: $$ I_{in} = \frac{V_{in} - 0}{R} = \frac{V_{in}}{R} $$
  2. Since op-amp draws zero input current, all of $I_{in}$ flows into the transistor collector: $I_C = I_{in}$.
  3. The collector current of an NPN transistor follows the Ebers-Moll equation ($V_{BE} \gg V_T$): $$ I_C \approx I_S\,e^{V_{BE}/V_T} $$ where $I_S$ is reverse saturation current and $V_T = \dfrac{kT}{q} \approx 26\text{ mV at }300\text{ K}$.
  4. Equating currents: $$ \frac{V_{in}}{R} = I_S\,e^{V_{BE}/V_T} \implies e^{V_{BE}/V_T} = \frac{V_{in}}{R\,I_S} $$
  5. Taking natural log ($\ln$) on both sides: $$ \frac{V_{BE}}{V_T} = \ln\!\left(\frac{V_{in}}{R\,I_S}\right) \implies V_{BE} = V_T\,\ln\!\left(\frac{V_{in}}{R\,I_S}\right) $$
  6. Since base is at $0\text{ V}$ and emitter is at $V_o$: $V_{BE} = V_B - V_E = 0 - V_o = -V_o$. $$ \boxed{V_o = -V_T\,\ln\!\left(\frac{V_{in}}{R\,I_S}\right) = -V_T\ln V_{in} + V_T\ln(R\,I_S)} $$

Conclusion: Output voltage is directly proportional to the logarithm of the input voltage. (Slope $\approx -60\text{ mV per decade}$ of input change).

Applications: (1) Compressing dynamic range, (2) Analog multipliers/dividers (using $\ln(A\cdot B) = \ln A + \ln B$), (3) Decibel meters, (4) Spectrophotometers (Beer-Lambert law: Absorbance $= -\log_{10}(I/I_0)$).

2.6 Charge Amplifier

💡 In Simple Terms: Piezoelectric crystals generate an electric charge $Q$ when squeezed. If you connect a long cable, the cable's own capacitance $C_{cable}$ steals charge, so your voltage reading depends on how long the wire is! A charge amplifier uses an op-amp integrator so that the sensor and cable sit at virtual ground ($0\text{ V}$). All charge is forced onto feedback capacitor $C_f$, making the reading completely independent of cable length! 
                   +---------||---------/\/\/---------+  Cf in parallel with Rf
                   |                    Rf            |
 Piezo Sensor Q    |                                  |
 + Cable Cc  ------+--------|\                        |
                            | - \                     |
                   GND -----|     >-------------------+---o Vo = -Q / Cf
                            | + /

Formula & Working

By virtual ground, no voltage builds across sensor capacitance $C_s$ or cable capacitance $C_c$. All charge $Q$ is transferred to feedback capacitor $C_f$:

$$ V_o = -\frac{Q}{C_f} $$

Since $Q = d\cdot F$ (where $d$ is piezoelectric constant in $\text{pC/N}$ and $F$ is dynamic force):

$$ V_o = -\frac{d}{C_f}\,F $$

The large feedback resistor $R_f$ prevents DC bias drift and establishes a low-frequency cutoff $f_L = \dfrac{1}{2\pi R_f C_f}$. (Cannot measure static DC force).

2.7 Phase-Sensitive Detector (PSD) / Lock-In Amplifier

💡 In Simple Terms: A synchronous switch driven by a reference clock. During the positive half of the reference clock, it passes the signal straight through ($+1$); during the negative half, it flips the signal upside down ($-1$). Any signal matching the reference frequency gets fully rectified into a steady DC voltage; random noise at other frequencies averages out to pure zero!

Working Principle & Formula

Input signal: $v_s(t) = V_m\sin(\omega t + \phi)$. Reference square wave has identical frequency $\omega$. Multiplying and averaging via low-pass filter gives:

$$ V_{dc} = \frac{2 V_m}{\pi}\cos\phi $$
  • If signal is in-phase ($\phi = 0^\circ$): $\cos 0^\circ = 1 \implies V_{dc} = +2V_m / \pi$ (Maximum Positive).
  • If signal is in quadrature ($\phi = 90^\circ$): $\cos 90^\circ = 0 \implies V_{dc} = 0$.
  • If signal is out-of-phase ($\phi = 180^\circ$): $\cos 180^\circ = -1 \implies V_{dc} = -2V_m / \pi$ (Maximum Negative).

Applications: LVDT secondary demodulation (identifies direction of core motion), carrier amplifier demodulators, and lock-in amplifiers detecting signals buried in $1000\times$ noise.

2.8 Actuators and Control Elements

💡 In Simple Terms: The controller is the brain, but the actuator is the muscle that turns electrical commands into physical force to open or close valves, move dampers, or drive conveyor belts.

1. Electrical Actuators

  • Solenoid: An electromagnetic coil with a spring-loaded soft iron plunger. Energising the coil creates magnetic pull force $F = \dfrac{1}{2}I^2\,\dfrac{dL}{dx}$. Plunger snaps in instantly. Used for on/off fluid valves, safety interlocks, and circuit breakers.
  • DC Servo Motor: Armature voltage control ($V = E_b + I_a R_a$, Torque $T = K_t I_a$). Paired with an optical encoder for high-precision robotic and valve positioning.
  • Stepper Motor: Rotates in discrete angular increments: Step angle $\theta_s = \dfrac{360^\circ}{m\,N_r}$ per pulse. Excellent for open-loop precision positioning without feedback.

2. Pneumatic Actuators

Most common in process plants. Uses compressed air ($3\text{ to }15\text{ psi}$ / $0.2\text{ to }1.0\text{ bar}$).

 Control Air (3-15 psi) ----> [ Diaphragm Chamber ]
                                    ||||| Flexible Diaphragm (Area Ad)
                                   -------
                                    \   / Opposing Range Spring (Stiffness k)
                                     \ /
                                      | Stem Travel x = (Ad / k) * P
                                      v
                              To Control Valve Plug

Stem travel is strictly proportional to air pressure: $x = \dfrac{A_d}{k}\,P$. Inherent explosion-proof safety, simple, reliable, and provides fail-safe action (Air-to-Open or Air-to-Close).

3. Hydraulic Actuators

Uses high-pressure incompressible fluid (oil at $50\text{ to }300\text{ bar}$) to generate massive forces in compact cylinder sizes ($F = P\cdot A$, Speed $v = Q/A$). System includes: reservoir, positive-displacement pump, pressure relief valve, directional spool valve, and hydraulic cylinder. Used in steel rolling mills, presses, and heavy turbine valves.

4. Control Valves and Inherent Characteristics

Flow equation through a control valve: $Q = C_v \sqrt{\dfrac{\Delta P}{G}}$ (where $C_v$ is valve flow coefficient and $G$ is specific gravity).

A charge amplifier is an inverting op-amp circuit with a feedback capacitor $C_f$ shunted by a high-value bleed resistor $R_f$, used with charge-generating transducers like piezoelectric crystals. Because the inverting terminal is maintained at virtual ground ($0\text{ V}$), all charge $Q$ generated by the crystal is forced onto feedback capacitor $C_f$:

$$ V_o = -\frac{Q}{C_f} $$

Significance: The output voltage is completely independent of the transducer capacitance and cable capacitance, eliminating cable-length calibration errors.

Explain the principle and operation of a phase-sensitive detector.

3 marks
Jun 2023 · Q4

A Phase-Sensitive Detector (PSD) measures the amplitude and phase of a periodic signal relative to a reference signal of identical frequency. A reference square wave drives an electronic switch: the signal is passed unmodified ($+1$) during the positive half-cycle and inverted ($-1$) during the negative half-cycle. Passing the result through a low-pass filter gives a DC voltage:

$$ V_{dc} = \frac{2 V_m}{\pi}\cos\phi $$

where $V_m$ is signal peak amplitude and $\phi$ is phase angle. The output is maximum positive when in-phase, zero in quadrature ($\phi = 90^\circ$), and negative when out-of-phase. Uncorrelated noise averages out to zero.

The signal is modulated and transmitted across an isolation barrier using magnetic (transformer), optical (opto-coupler), or capacitive coupling. Q. Derive an expression for the output voltage of a logarithmic amplifier and show that it is proportional to the logarithm of input voltage.

8 marks
Jun 2023 · Q13(a)Apr 2026 · Q13(a)

1. Circuit Diagram

                   +------- NPN Transistor Q (Collector to node, Base to GND) ---+
                   |                                                             |
 Vin o-----/\/\/---+--------|\                                                   |
            R      |        | - \                                                |
                   +--------|     >----------------------------------------------+--o Output Vo
                            | + /
                     GND ---|/

2. Circuit Assumptions

  • The op-amp is ideal: open-loop gain $A_{ol} \to \infty$, input impedance $R_{in} \to \infty$, input bias currents $I_B = 0$.
  • By virtual short, the inverting terminal is held at virtual ground ($0\text{ V}$).
  • Input voltage $V_{in} > 0$, biasing the NPN transistor in its forward-active region.

3. Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

Step 1: Input Current Expression
Current flowing through input resistor $R$ is: $$ I_{in} = \frac{V_{in} - 0}{R} = \frac{V_{in}}{R} $$
Step 2: Feedback Current Expression
Since op-amp drawing current is zero, KCL at inverting node dictates that all input current enters the transistor collector: $$ I_C = I_{in} = \frac{V_{in}}{R} $$
Step 3: Transistor Collector Current Physics
Collector current in forward-active mode follows the diode equation: $$ I_C = I_S\left(e^{V_{BE}/V_T} - 1\right) \approx I_S\,e^{V_{BE}/V_T} \qquad (\text{for } V_{BE} \gg V_T) $$ where $I_S$ is reverse saturation current and $V_T = \dfrac{kT}{q}$ is thermal voltage ($\approx 26\text{ mV at }300\text{ K}$).
Step 4: Solve for Base-Emitter Voltage $V_{BE}$
Equating the expressions: $$ \frac{V_{in}}{R} = I_S\,e^{V_{BE}/V_T} \implies e^{V_{BE}/V_T} = \frac{V_{in}}{R\,I_S} $$ Taking natural log ($\ln$) of both sides: $$ \frac{V_{BE}}{V_T} = \ln\!\left(\frac{V_{in}}{R\,I_S}\right) \implies V_{BE} = V_T\,\ln\!\left(\frac{V_{in}}{R\,I_S}\right) $$
Step 5: Relate $V_{BE}$ to Output Voltage $V_o$
Since the transistor base is tied to ground ($V_B = 0$) and emitter is tied to op-amp output ($V_E = V_o$): $$ V_{BE} = V_B - V_E = 0 - V_o = -V_o \implies V_o = -V_{BE} $$ Substituting $V_{BE}$: $$ \boxed{V_o = -V_T\,\ln\!\left(\frac{V_{in}}{R\,I_S}\right) = -V_T\ln V_{in} + V_T\ln(R\,I_S)} $$

Conclusion: Since $V_T, R,$ and $I_S$ are constants for a fixed temperature, the output voltage $V_o$ is strictly proportional to $\ln(V_{in})$. Converting to common logarithm ($\log_{10}$):

$$ V_o = -2.303\,V_T\,\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{V_{in}}{R\,I_S}\right) \approx -60\text{ mV per decade} $$

             |  >--+--- R1 ---+------/\/\/----+
          +--+/    |          |               |   |\ A3
          |        Rg(gain)   +--- Vo1        +---| - \
          |        |                              |     >---o Vo

  3. Since op-amp inputs draw zero current, the same current $I_G$ flows through both $R_1$ resistors: $$ V_{o1} = V_1 + I_G R_1 = V_1 + \left(\frac{V_1 - V_2}{R_G}\right)R_1 $$ $$ V_{o2} = V_2 - I_G R_1 = V_2 - \left(\frac{V_1 - V_2}{R_G}\right)R_1 $$

Q. Explain the operation of Half-Wave and Full-Wave Precision Rectifiers with circuit analysis.

1. Half-Wave Precision Rectifier

                        Diode D
 Vin o-------+\           |\|
             |  >---------| >|---+--------o Output Vo
        +----|-/          |/|    |
        |    |                   |       RL
        +----+-------------------+------/\/\/--- GND
  • Positive Half-Cycle ($V_{in} > 0$): Op-amp output goes positive $\implies$ Diode is forward-biased $\implies$ Feedback loop is closed through diode $\implies$ Virtual short forces $V_o = V_{in}$. The $0.7\text{ V}$ diode drop is overcome by the op-amp output swinging to $V_{in} + 0.7\text{ V}$.
  • Negative Half-Cycle ($V_{in} < 0$): Op-amp output swings negative $\implies$ Diode is reverse-biased $\implies$ Loop opens $\implies$ Zero current flows through $R_L \implies V_o = 0$.
$$ V_o = \begin{cases} V_{in}, & V_{in} > 0 \\ 0, & V_{in} \le 0 \end{cases} \qquad V_{avg} = \frac{V_m}{\pi} $$

2. Full-Wave Precision Rectifier (Absolute Value Circuit)

              Stage 1: Inverting Half-Wave Rectifier            Stage 2: Summing Inverter
                           D1
    Vin o---+---/\/\/---+---|<-+                     Vin o-----/\/\/----+
            |     R     |  [A1]|                          R      |  |\ A2
            |           +---|>-+---+ V1              V1 o-----/\/\/----+| - \
            |              D2                                R/2        |     >---o Vo = |Vin|
            +-----------------------------------------------------------| + /
                                                                        |/  (Rf = R)

Stage 1: Generates inverting half-wave rectified voltage $V_1$:

$$ V_1 = \begin{cases} -V_{in}, & V_{in} > 0 \\ 0, & V_{in} \le 0 \end{cases} $$

Stage 2: An inverting summing amplifier combining $V_{in}$ (via resistor $R$) and $V_1$ (via resistor $R/2$):

$$ V_o = -\left[\frac{R}{R} V_{in} + \frac{R}{R/2} V_1\right] = -(V_{in} + 2 V_1) $$
  • When $V_{in} > 0$: $V_1 = -V_{in} \implies V_o = -[V_{in} + 2(-V_{in})] = -( -V_{in} ) = +V_{in}$.
  • When $V_{in} < 0$: $V_1 = 0 \implies V_o = -[V_{in} + 0] = -V_{in} = |V_{in}|$.

Conclusion: For both positive and negative inputs, $V_o = |V_{in}|$. Full-wave rectification is achieved down to microvolt signal levels!

Q. Explain Phase-Sensitive Detectors (PSD) and their significance in industrial instrumentation.

6 marks

A Phase-Sensitive Detector is an electronic circuit that provides a DC output proportional to both the amplitude $V_m$ of an AC input signal and the cosine of its phase difference $\phi$ relative to an AC reference signal of the same frequency.

                   Reference Square Wave (Frequency w)
                            |
  Input AC Signal -------->[ S ]----> Polarity Switched Output ---->[ Low-Pass Filter ]----> DC Output V_dc
  Vs = Vm*sin(wt + phi)    (+1 / -1)                                 (Averaging)            V_dc = (2Vm/pi)*cos(phi)

Mathematical Formulation

The reference signal switches an analog multiplier/switch between $+1$ and $-1$. Integrating over a half-cycle:

$$ V_{dc} = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\pi V_m\sin(\theta + \phi)\,d\theta = \frac{2 V_m}{\pi}\cos\phi $$

Significance in Instrumentation

  1. Polarity & Direction Discrimination: Output voltage is positive for $\phi = 0^\circ$ and negative for $\phi = 180^\circ$. In LVDT displacement sensors, this detects whether the core moved left or right.
  2. Quadrature Rejection: Harmonics and reactive out-of-phase noise components ($\phi = 90^\circ$) evaluate to $\cos(90^\circ) = 0$ and are completely cancelled out.
  3. Weak Signal Recovery (Lock-In Amplification): Extracts tiny sensor signals buried deeply in ambient noise ($1000\times$ larger), because uncorrelated noise averages out to zero over time.

Q. Describe various electrical actuators / Explain the operation of any two actuators with diagrams and industrial applications.

1. Solenoid Actuator

         Coil Windings                  Plunger (Soft Iron)
        +---||||||||||---+            +-----------------+
        |   ||||||||||   |=== Air ===>|                 |===> Stroke Output (Linear)
        +---||||||||||---+  Magnetic  +-----------------+
          Current I In        Pull        Opposing Return Spring

Principle & Working: A copper coil surrounds a movable soft iron plunger. When current $I$ energises the coil, magnetic flux creates an axial pulling force: $F = \dfrac{1}{2}I^2\,\dfrac{dL}{dx}$. The plunger snaps inward against a return spring. When power is removed, the spring returns the plunger. Applications: Solenoid shut-off valves for natural gas and water, emergency trip valves, door locks.

2. DC Servo Motor Actuator

Principle & Working: Operates on Lorentz force ($T = K_t I_a$, back-EMF $E_b = K_e \omega$). An internal optical encoder continuously feeds back shaft position to a closed-loop servo amplifier. Armature voltage control precisely adjusts speed and position without hunting. Applications: Motorised control valves, robot joint drives, CNC machine tool axis drives.

3. Stepper Motor Actuator

Principle: Converts digital electrical pulses into precise mechanical angular increments. Step angle: $\theta_s = \dfrac{360^\circ}{m\,N_r}$. Operates open-loop without expensive feedback sensors. Applications: Precise chemical dosing pump drives, automated optical positioners, 3D printers.

4. Pneumatic Diaphragm Actuator

Principle: Instrument air pressure ($3\text{ to }15\text{ psi}$) acts on a flexible rubber diaphragm of area $A_d$ opposed by a heavy coil spring of stiffness $k$. Stem travel is $x = \dfrac{A_d}{k} P$. Provides fail-safe operation (Air-to-Open or Air-to-Close). Applications: Steam and chemical control valves throughout oil refineries and thermal power plants.

Q. Illustrate hydraulic actuating systems with components used in industries.

Hydraulic systems transmit power using an incompressible pressurised liquid (hydraulic oil at $50\text{ to }300\text{ bar}$) to generate enormous forces in compact mechanisms.

 [ Oil Reservoir ]---->[ Hydraulic Pump ]---->[ Pressure Relief Valve ]---->[ Directional Control Valve ]
         ^                                                                               |
         |                                                                        Forward / Reverse Lines
         |                                                                               v
         +----------------------- Return Line <----------------------------------[ Hydraulic Cylinder ]
                                                                                  Force F = P * A

Key System Components

  1. Reservoir (Tank): Stores hydraulic fluid, cools the oil, and allows trapped air and debris to settle.
  2. Hydraulic Pump: Positive displacement gear or piston pump driven by electric motor; converts mechanical power into fluid pressure flow ($P\cdot Q$).
  3. Pressure Relief Valve: Safety device that bypasses oil back to tank if pressure exceeds maximum threshold.
  4. Directional Control Valve (DCV): Solenoid-actuated spool valve that directs high-pressure oil to the cap-end or rod-end of the cylinder.
  5. Hydraulic Cylinder (Actuator): Produces linear mechanical force: $F = P\cdot A$ and speed $v = Q / A$.

Industrial Applications: Heavy metal forging presses, steel rolling mill screw-down mechanisms, injection moulding clamping units, and high-pressure steam turbine governor valves.

Q. Write short notes on fluid valves and their characteristics.

4 – 6 marks
Jun 2023 · Q14(b)Apr 2026 · Q14(b)

Control valves are the most common final control elements in process automation. A valve varies its internal orifice restriction to adjust fluid flow rate $Q = C_v \sqrt{\Delta P / G}$ (where $C_v$ is valve flow coefficient).

1. Major Valve Body Types

  • Globe Valve: Linear stem movement, high throttling precision, excellent shut-off; standard for process loops.
  • Ball Valve: Quarter-turn spherical plug with hole; low pressure drop, high capacity, ideal for slurry and tight shut-off.
  • Butterfly Valve: Rotating disc; compact, low cost, used for large-diameter water and gas pipelines.
  • Diaphragm Valve: Flexible membrane seals fluid; isolates corrosive and pharmaceutical fluids.

2. Inherent Flow Characteristics

The relationship between percentage valve travel ($x/x_{max}$) and percentage flow ($Q/Q_{max}$) at constant pressure drop:

  1. Quick-Opening: Large flow increase at initial travel; used for on/off and safety dumping.
  2. Linear: Flow is directly proportional to stem stroke ($\dfrac{Q}{Q_{max}} = \dfrac{x}{x_{max}}$); used for liquid level loops.
  3. Equal Percentage: Equal increments of stem travel produce equal percentage changes of the existing flow: $$ \frac{Q}{Q_{max}} = R^{(x/x_{max} - 1)} $$ (where $R$ is rangeability $\approx 20\text{–}50$). Compensates for piping pressure drops and is the industry standard for temperature and pressure control loops!
