Part 1 · Syllabus Theory

3.1 Cable transmission of analog and digital data

In an industrial plant, the sensor and the controller are often hundreds of metres apart, so the measurement signal must be transmitted reliably through cables. The simplest and still most common method is analog signal transmission in which the measured value is represented by the amplitude of a voltage or current. Current loops (4–20 mA) are preferred over voltage signals because current is the same at every point of the series loop, so cable resistance and moderate voltage pick-up do not cause errors, the receiver has low impedance (250 Ω converts the signal into 1–5 V), and the "live zero" of 4 mA allows detection of open-circuit faults (0 mA = broken wire) and powers two-wire transmitters from the loop. Voltage transmission (0–5 V, 0–10 V) is used over short distances. Cable types are twisted pair (cancels magnetic interference), shielded twisted pair (electrostatic shielding, shield grounded at one end to avoid ground loops) and coaxial cable (high-frequency signals).

Digital data transmission sends information as binary codes (bits) over a cable, either in parallel (many wires, short distance, e.g. IEEE-488/GPIB) or serial form (one pair of wires, long distances). Common serial standards are RS-232 (single-ended, up to about 15 m, 20 kbps), RS-422 (differential, up to 1.2 km, one driver and ten receivers) and RS-485 (differential, multi-drop up to 32 nodes, 1.2 km, up to 10 Mbps), as well as Ethernet over twisted pair. Digital transmission is immune to noise as long as 0 and 1 are distinguishable, can use error-detection codes (parity, CRC), allows multiplexing of many variables on one cable, and carries diagnostic information. It requires A/D and D/A conversion and protocols.

3.2 Fibre-optic data transmission

An optical fibre transmits data as pulses of light through a thin strand of glass or plastic. A fibre has a central core of refractive index $n_1$ surrounded by a cladding of slightly lower index $n_2$ ($n_1>n_2$) and an outer protective jacket. Light injected into the core within the acceptance angle is guided by total internal reflection at the core–cladding boundary. The critical angle and numerical aperture are

$$ \theta_c=\sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right),\qquad \text{NA}=\sin\theta_a=\sqrt{n_1^{2}-n_2^{2}} . $$

The link has three parts: (i) a transmitter – an LED (cheap, short distance, multimode) or laser diode (high power, narrow spectrum, long distance) which converts the electrical signal into light (intensity modulation); (ii) the fibre cable – multimode step-index, multimode graded-index or single-mode; and (iii) a receiver – a PIN photodiode or avalanche photodiode that converts light back into an electrical signal, followed by an amplifier and decision circuit. Repeaters are inserted after long runs to counter attenuation, given by $\alpha=\dfrac{10}{L}\log_{10}\dfrac{P_{in}}{P_{out}}$ dB/km (about 0.2 dB/km for single-mode at 1550 nm).

 Electrical    +-----------+   light   +----------------------+   light   +-----------+  Electrical
 data  ------>| LED/LASER |--========>| core | cladding|jacket |========-->| PIN /APD  |------> data
               |  driver   |           +----------------------+           | + amp     |
               +-----------+            total internal reflection         +-----------+

Advantages: immunity to electromagnetic interference and lightning (ideal near motors and high-voltage plant), very large bandwidth and data rate, low attenuation over long distances, galvanic isolation, no sparks (safe in explosive atmospheres), small size, light weight and security against tapping. Disadvantages: higher cost of terminals, difficult jointing/splicing, fragility and bend loss, and need for electro-optical conversion.

3.3 Pneumatic transmission

In pneumatic transmission, the measured value is represented by the pressure of air in a tube, with a standard range of 3–15 psi (20–100 kPa), which is conveyed through small-bore tubing (6 mm) from the transmitter to the controller or valve positioner. The 3 psi "live zero" detects failures. The transmitter is typically a flapper–nozzle mechanism: a flapper positioned by the measurement (via bellows or force-balance beam) restricts the escape of air from a nozzle that is supplied through a fixed restriction from a 20 psi air supply; the back-pressure in the nozzle changes with the flapper gap, and is amplified by a pneumatic relay (booster) to give the 3–15 psi output.

Merits: intrinsically safe (no electric spark) so used in explosive/flammable atmospheres, simple and rugged, inexpensive, directly drives valve actuators. Demerits: limited transmission distance (a few hundred metres) because of the time lag from air compressibility and tube resistance and capacitance (speed of propagation is of the order of the speed of sound, about 300 m/s), need of clean, dry compressed air, leakage, slow response and difficulty interfacing with computers (needs I/P and P/I converters).

3.4 Process control networks – functions and general characteristics

A process control network is the communication infrastructure that links field instruments (sensors, transmitters, actuators), controllers (PLCs/DCS), operator stations and supervisory computers in an automated plant. Its functions are to: collect measurement data from the field; deliver control commands to final control elements; allow configuration, calibration and diagnostics of devices; share data between controllers and the supervisory/business level (historians, MES/ERP); synchronise time and events; manage alarms; and provide secure, deterministic communication.

General characteristics of such networks are: real-time/deterministic response (guaranteed maximum delay), reliability and redundancy (dual cables and controllers), noise immunity suitable for an industrial environment, topology (bus, star, ring or tree), scalability and easy expansion, interoperability through open standards, distance and speed sufficient to cover the plant, security (isolation from the corporate/IT network and access control), intrinsic safety in hazardous areas, and low cost of cabling by multi-drop wiring. Commonly the plant network is arranged as a hierarchy: field level (sensor bus/fieldbus), control level (PLC/DCS network), supervisory level (SCADA/Ethernet) and enterprise level.

Fieldbus

Fieldbus is the all-digital, two-way, multi-drop serial communication system that replaces 4–20 mA point-to-point wiring by connecting many field devices on one cable. Its advantages are large saving in wiring, rich information (device status and diagnostics), multi-variable transmission, remote configuration, and distributed control in the field devices themselves. FOUNDATION Fieldbus H1 works at 31.25 kbit/s over twisted pair with bus power and intrinsically safe options (up to 32 devices per segment, 1900 m), and the high-speed Ethernet (HSE) at 100 Mbit/s links H1 segments to the control system. The physical layer, data link layer and application layer (with a user layer containing function blocks like AI, PID, AO) are defined.

Profibus

PROFIBUS (Process Field Bus; IEC 61158/EN 50170) is a widely used fieldbus standard with master–slave and token-passing methods. Profibus-DP (Decentralised Peripherals) is high-speed (9.6 kbit/s up to 12 Mbit/s) over RS-485 for factory automation, connecting a PLC to remote I/O, drives and sensors (up to 126 stations). Profibus-PA (Process Automation) uses the same protocol on the intrinsically safe MBP physical layer (31.25 kbit/s) with bus power for process and hazardous areas, and Profibus-FMS is for peer-to-peer communication between controllers. The comparison with Foundation Fieldbus: Profibus is mainly master–slave with cyclic polling and strong in the factory automation and drives sector, whereas Foundation Fieldbus supports distributed control with function blocks in the field devices and scheduled communication, and is stronger in process industries.

3.5 Radio, wireless communication and WLAN architecture

Radio communication transmits information with electromagnetic waves (typically VHF/UHF, 150–900 MHz or 2.4 GHz) from a transmitter to a receiver, usually point-to-point or point-to-multipoint (telemetry) with dedicated radio modems and licensed or licence-free bands. It is used in SCADA for remote, unmanned sites such as pipelines and water networks. Wireless communication in the broader sense covers all media without wires – radio, microwave, infra-red, satellite, cellular (GSM/4G/5G), Wi-Fi (WLAN), Bluetooth and ZigBee / WirelessHART/ISA100 for sensor networks – and normally implies a networked, protocol-based system with addressing, error control and often mesh routing.

WLAN architecture

A wireless LAN (IEEE 802.11) links devices without cables. Its main components are the stations (STA) – wireless clients such as laptops, HMI tablets, PLC modules; the access point (AP) – a bridge between the wireless medium and the wired distribution system (DS); the basic service set (BSS) – the group of stations around one AP, in infrastructure mode, or an independent BSS (IBSS / ad hoc mode) in which stations talk directly; the extended service set (ESS) – several BSSs connected by a common distribution system (Ethernet), allowing roaming; and a portal to other wired networks. Medium access uses CSMA/CA (carrier-sense multiple access with collision avoidance) with acknowledgements, and security is by WPA2/WPA3 encryption.

3.6 Virtual instrumentation – architecture, comparison and graphical programming

A virtual instrument (VI) is a combination of a general-purpose computer, modular measurement hardware and software, which together perform the function of a traditional stand-alone instrument. The defining idea is "the software is the instrument": the user defines the functionality of the measurement system by software, instead of the manufacturer burning it in hardware. National Instruments' LabVIEW is the best known development tool.

Architecture of a virtual instrument

 +-----------+   +----------------+   +---------------+   +-----------------------------------+
 | Sensors / |-->| Signal         |-->| DAQ hardware  |-->| COMPUTER (PC)                     |
 | process   |   | conditioning   |   | (ADC, DAC,    |   |  Drivers  -> Application software |
 | actuators |<--| (amp, filter)  |<--|  DIO, counter)|<--|  (LabVIEW): analysis, display,    |
 +-----------+   +----------------+   +---------------+   |  storage, network                 |
                                                           +-----------------------------------+

The VI architecture consists of the following blocks. (1) Sensors/transducers pick up physical variables. (2) Signal conditioning hardware (SCXI/modules) amplifies, filters, isolates and linearises the signals. (3) Data-acquisition (DAQ) hardware – plug-in cards or external modules (PCI, USB, PXI) containing multiplexers, sample-and-hold, A/D and D/A converters, digital I/O and counters/timers; the sampling theorem $f_s \ge 2 f_{max}$ must be satisfied and resolution is $\text{LSB}=V_{FS}/2^{n}$. (4) Computer providing processor, memory, display, storage, and networking. (5) Driver software (e.g. NI-DAQmx, VISA) that links hardware and application. (6) Application software (LabVIEW, LabWindows/CVI) that gives the user-defined measurement logic, signal analysis (FFT, filtering, statistics), presentation (graphs, indicators) and report generation. A VI has a front panel (user interface: controls and indicators) and a block diagram (graphical source code), plus an icon/connector pane that lets it be used as a sub-VI.

Graphical programming

In graphical programming (G language in LabVIEW) a program is created by placing graphical icons (functions, sub-VIs) on a block diagram and connecting them by wires that carry data. The execution is governed by dataflow: a node executes only when all its inputs have data, and outputs data to the next node as soon as it finishes, so independent branches run in parallel automatically. The key features are: intuitive "drag-and-drop" development, a rich library of acquisition, analysis, and I/O functions, structures (For Loop, While Loop, Case, Sequence), shift registers, clusters and arrays, automatic parallelism (multi-threading), easy creation of the front-panel user interface, and fast debugging (highlight execution, probes). In contrast, sequential (text-based) programming (C, BASIC) executes statements line by line in a defined order, needs knowledge of syntax, and is slower to develop for measurement applications.

Q. Explain fibre-optic data transmission.

8 marks
Jun 2023 · Q15(a)Apr 2026 · Q15(a)

Fibre-optic data transmission is the process of sending information as modulated light through a thin glass or plastic fibre. The system comprises an optical transmitter, the optical fibre, (optional repeaters/couplers) and an optical receiver.

 Electrical     +--------------+     +---------------------+     +--------------+    Electrical
 signal  -----> | LED / Laser  |====>| Optical fibre       |====>| Photodetector|---> signal
 (data)         | + driver     |     | core | cladding     |     | PIN/APD + amp|
                +--------------+     +---------------------+     +--------------+
                  Transmitter          Total internal reflection         Receiver

Transmitter. The electrical data is applied to a driver circuit that modulates the intensity of an LED (850 or 1300 nm, low cost, short links, multimode fibre) or a laser diode (1310/1550 nm, higher power, narrow line width, long links, single-mode fibre). Fibre. It consists of a high-index core (8–9 µm for single-mode; 50 or 62.5 µm for multimode), a lower-index cladding (125 µm overall) and a protective buffer/jacket. Light entering within the acceptance angle ($\text{NA}=\sqrt{n_1^{2}-n_2^{2}}$) undergoes repeated total internal reflection at the core–cladding interface (critical angle $\theta_c=\sin^{-1}(n_2/n_1)$) and travels along the fibre. Fibres are step-index multimode (large dispersion, short distance), graded-index multimode (reduced modal dispersion) and single-mode (lowest dispersion and attenuation; used for long distance and high speed). Signal power decreases as it propagates by $P_{out}=P_{in}\,10^{-\alpha L/10}$ ($\alpha$ in dB/km), and dispersion broadens pulses, limiting bit rate; repeaters or optical amplifiers regenerate the signal. Receiver. A PIN photodiode or avalanche photodiode converts the light into a photocurrent, which is amplified (transimpedance amplifier) and passed through a comparator to recover digital data.

In industry, fibre is used for plant backbone networks, communication in electrically noisy areas (near drives, welders, high-voltage substations), in explosive atmospheres, and for long cable runs between control room and remote I/O. Advantages: immunity from EMI/RFI and lightning, very wide bandwidth (Gbps), low loss, electrical isolation (no ground loops), small size and light weight, security, no sparks. Disadvantages: costly terminal equipment, specialised splicing, fragile, bending loss, needs electro-optical conversion.

Q. Explain pneumatic transmission.

8 marks
Jun 2023 · Q16(a)

Pneumatic transmission is a method of transmitting process information, over a distance, by the pressure of compressed air within a tube. The standard signal range is 3–15 psi (0.2–1.0 bar, 20–100 kPa), where 3 psi represents 0 % of the measured range and 15 psi represents 100 %. The live zero allows detection of leaks or tube failures, and helps in overcoming friction and dead-band.

   Supply air 20 psi
        |
      [Restrictor]----+----> to relay (booster) --> 3-15 psi output signal
                      |
                  [Nozzle]  <-- gap controlled by --  [Flapper] <-- bellows / force beam <-- measured variable

Working (flapper–nozzle transmitter). Clean, dry, regulated air at about 20 psi passes through a fixed restriction (orifice) to a nozzle. A flapper, positioned by the measured variable through a bellows, diaphragm or a force-balance beam, covers the nozzle. When the flapper is close to the nozzle, air escape is reduced, so back-pressure rises toward the supply pressure; when it moves away, the back-pressure falls. Hence the nozzle back-pressure $P_b$ is a non-linear but monotonic function of the flapper gap, which is made nearly linear over a small range (about 0.001 inch). Since the back-pressure has very small air flow capacity, a pneumatic relay/booster amplifies the volume flow and delivers the standard 3–15 psi signal through the transmission tubing to the controller (a similar nozzle–flapper unit) or to a valve actuator. A force-balance or feedback bellows is used to linearise the output and reduce the motion required.

The transmission lag arises from the resistance $R$ of the tube and the capacitance $C$ of the air volume, so a tube of length $L$ behaves like a first-order lag with $\tau=RC$, increasing roughly with $L^{2}$; typical practical limit is about 100–300 m (longer with relays). Advantages: intrinsically safe (no electric spark) for hazardous areas, simple, robust, inexpensive, easily maintained, and compatible with pneumatic control valves. Disadvantages: time lag and limited distance, need for clean dry air supply, leaks, low accuracy and slow response, difficulty in interfacing with computers (needs I/P and P/I converters), and tubing is bulkier than wire.

Q. Compare radio and wireless communication.

Radio communication transmits information using radio-frequency electromagnetic waves between a transmitter and receiver, typically as point-to-point or broadcast links, whereas the term wireless communication covers all kinds of transmission without a physical conductor – radio, microwave, infrared, satellite, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, ZigBee and cellular networks. Radio is therefore one of the technologies of wireless communication. In industrial use, radio telemetry (VHF/UHF) is used to link remote RTUs with a master station, while wireless networks (WLAN, WirelessHART, ISA100.11a) connect large numbers of field sensors and mobile devices.

Q. List the advantages of wireless communication.

4 marks
Apr 2026 · Q16(b)

Wireless communication replaces cables with radio links, which offers several advantages for industrial automation. (1) Reduced installation cost and time – no cable trenching, conduits and cable trays; very important in large, remote or already-built plants. (2) Access to difficult or hazardous locations – rotating machines, mobile vehicles, remote tanks, pipelines, offshore sites. (3) Mobility and flexibility – operators with tablets can receive data from anywhere; instruments can be relocated easily; networks are quickly expandable. (4) Immunity to cable faults such as breaks, corrosion, ground loops and lightning-induced damage, and low maintenance. (5) Scalability with mesh networks adding self-healing paths and redundancy. (6) Rapid deployment for temporary or trial measurements and lower cost for monitoring of non-critical points that previously were not economical to wire. (7) Support of advanced applications such as asset monitoring, predictive maintenance and the Industrial IoT. Limitations include interference, finite battery life of nodes, and cybersecurity concerns.

Q. Explain the architecture of a virtual instrumentation system with block diagram.

A virtual instrument is a system in which a general-purpose computer, together with modular measurement hardware and graphical software, takes over the functions of a conventional instrument. Its architecture can be seen as a chain from the physical world to the user.

 PHYSICAL      +-------------+    +---------------+    +----------------+    +------------------------+
 WORLD   ----> |  SENSORS    |--->| SIGNAL        |--->| DAQ HARDWARE   |--->|  PERSONAL COMPUTER     |
 (temp,        | transducers |    | CONDITIONING  |    | MUX, S/H, ADC  |    |  +------------------+  |
 pressure,     +-------------+    | amp, filter,  |    | DAC, DIO, timer|    |  | Driver software  |  |
 flow...)  <----  ACTUATORS <-----| isolation     |<---|  (PCI/USB/PXI) |<---|  +------------------+  |
                                  +---------------+    +----------------+    |  | Application s/w  |  |
                                                                             |  | (LabVIEW) : front|  |
                                                                             |  | panel + diagram  |  |
                                                                             |  +------------------+  |
                                                                             +-----------+------------+
                                                                       Display / storage / network / printer

1. Sensors and transducers convert the physical quantity (temperature, pressure, strain, speed…) to an electrical signal; actuators carry the control signals back to the process. 2. Signal conditioning – amplification (to match the ADC range), filtering (anti-aliasing), linearisation, isolation, cold-junction compensation, excitation for bridges. 3. DAQ hardware – a multiplexer selects among channels; a sample-and-hold circuit freezes the value; the ADC converts it into a number of resolution $n$ bits, so the smallest step is $V_{FS}/2^{n}$; sampling must satisfy $f_s\ge 2f_{max}$ (Nyquist). DAQ cards also contain D/A converters for outputs, digital I/O for switches and relays, and counters/timers. They are connected to the PC via PCI, PCIe, USB, Ethernet or PXI backplanes; for instruments GPIB, RS-232 and VXI interfaces exist. 4. Computer – the processor, memory, hard disk, display and network connection supply the computing power. 5. Driver software (NI-DAQmx, VISA, IVI) hides the hardware details and gives standard function calls. 6. Application software – LabVIEW/LabWindows/Visual Basic – defines the front panel and the program logic: acquisition, processing (FFT, filtering, statistics, PID control), display, data logging and communication with databases, web or other instruments. The VI has a front panel (the user interface with controls and indicators that emulate a physical instrument panel) and a block diagram (graphical code), enabling the user to build a custom instrument quickly. Because hardware is generic, a multimeter, an oscilloscope, a spectrum analyser and a data logger can all be implemented on the same hardware just by changing the software.

Q. Explain the concepts and key features of graphical programming / Compare graphical programming with sequential programming.

4 – 8 marks
May 2024 · Q16(a) (8)Apr 2025 · Q16(b) (7)Model Q15(b)

Concept. Graphical programming is a way of writing programs by drawing instead of typing. In LabVIEW's "G" language the programmer builds a block diagram from icons representing functions (arithmetic, DAQ, analysis, file I/O), structures and sub-VIs, and connects them with wires which carry data of particular types (colour-coded, e.g. orange for floating-point numbers, green for Booleans, pink for strings). A front panel with controls (inputs) and indicators (outputs) forms the user interface. Execution follows dataflow: a node executes only when data is available at all its inputs, and it passes its results to the next node when finished. Unlike text languages there is no line-by-line order; therefore, parallel branches execute concurrently, giving natural multitasking and multi-threading.

Key features. (1) Intuitive, visual, drag-and-drop development and quick learning for engineers; (2) built-in libraries for acquisition, instrument control, signal analysis, mathematics and communication; (3) structures – For/While loops, Case, Sequence, Event structures; shift registers and local variables; (4) hierarchical modularity through sub-VIs; (5) automatic parallelism and memory management; (6) integrated debugging with execution highlighting, probes and breakpoints; (7) shorter development time and easy interface creation; (8) ability to call text-based code and to compile to real-time or FPGA targets.

Q. Discuss process control networks used in industries / explain different types of communication networks used for data collection and control.

6 – 7 marks
Apr 2025 · Q16(a) (7)May 2024 · Q15(b) (functions, 4) & Q16(b) (two networks, 6)Model Q16(a)

A process control network connects the various levels of automation in an industry so that data collected from field devices can be used for control and supervision, and so that control commands can be sent back. The networks form a hierarchy:

 Enterprise level  : Ethernet / TCP-IP  (ERP, MES, historian)
 Supervisory level : SCADA / DCS operator stations  -- Ethernet, OPC
 Control level     : PLC / DCS controllers          -- Control network (Ethernet/IP, Profinet, Modbus TCP)
 Field level       : Sensors, transmitters, valves  -- 4-20 mA / HART / Fieldbus / Profibus / AS-i

Functions (answer for five-function question): (1) real-time data acquisition from field devices; (2) transmitting control commands to actuators; (3) operator supervision – displays, alarms, trending; (4) device configuration, calibration and diagnostics; (5) data exchange with historians and enterprise systems; (6) time synchronisation and sequence-of-event recording; (7) redundancy and security.

Types of networks used:

  • Point-to-point analog (4–20 mA, HART): each device is wired to the controller on its own pair; HART superimposes digital data (FSK at 1200/2200 Hz) on the analog signal for configuration and diagnostics.
  • Fieldbus (Foundation Fieldbus H1, Profibus-PA, DeviceNet, CAN): multi-drop digital bus connecting several field devices over a single cable; reduces wiring and enables distributed control and diagnostics. Foundation Fieldbus H1 runs at 31.25 kbit/s.
  • Profibus-DP / Modbus (RS-485): master–slave polling between PLCs and remote I/O and drives, up to 12 Mbit/s on Profibus-DP; Modbus RTU at typically 9.6–115 kbit/s.
  • Industrial Ethernet (Ethernet/IP, Profinet, Modbus TCP): high-speed (100 Mbit/s–1 Gbit/s) network between PLCs, HMIs, SCADA and corporate systems, with switches and redundant rings.
  • Wireless networks (WirelessHART, ISA100, Wi-Fi, radio modems): for remote or mobile devices where wiring is costly.

The general network characteristics are deterministic response, noise immunity, redundancy, scalability, interoperability by open standards, security (segmentation with firewalls so that the control network is isolated from the Internet), and intrinsic safety. Topologies used are bus (fieldbus), star (switched Ethernet), ring (redundant Ethernet) and tree.

Q. Explain Fieldbus / How does Profibus help in industrial data communication? / Compare Profibus and Fieldbus.

3 – 4 marks
May 2024 · Q6Model paper Q5 & Q16(b)

Fieldbus is a digital, bi-directional, multi-drop communication network that connects field devices – transmitters, valves, drives – with controllers in place of individual 4–20 mA wires. All devices share the same cable, which also supplies power and (with suitable barriers) enables intrinsic safety. The devices contain microprocessors, so they send not only the process value but also status, diagnostics and configuration data, and can execute control function blocks (PID) themselves, giving control in the field. Foundation Fieldbus H1 works at 31.25 kbit/s on up to 1900 m with 32 devices per segment.

Profibus helps industrial data communication by providing a standardised (IEC 61158) high-speed serial bus: Profibus-DP (up to 12 Mbit/s, RS-485) links PLCs to distributed I/O, drives and sensors in factory automation with cyclic master–slave polling and token passing among masters; Profibus-PA (31.25 kbit/s, intrinsically safe) is used for process instruments; Profibus-FMS for cell-level communication. Its advantages are reduced wiring, fast deterministic response, vendor interoperability, simple diagnostics, and hot-swapping of devices.
