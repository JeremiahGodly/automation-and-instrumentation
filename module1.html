Part 1 · Syllabus Theory (Simplified & Clear)

1.1 Introduction to Process Control

💡 In Simple Terms: Think of taking a shower. You want comfortable warm water (Set Point). Your skin feels if it's too hot or cold (Sensor). Your brain compares how it feels to what you want (Error Detector). If it's too cold, your brain calculates how much to turn the handle (Controller), and your hand turns the hot water knob (Final Control Element / Actuator). An industrial control loop does this exact same job—automatically, continuously, and accurately!

Definitions:

  • Process (Plant): Any physical operation or system where variables like temperature, pressure, fluid level, or chemical concentration are changed or maintained (e.g., steam boiler, chemical reactor, storage tank).
  • Process Control: The automatic monitoring and regulation of critical process variables to keep them at desired target values (set points) regardless of external disturbances.

Elements of the Control Loop

  1. Controlled Variable ($c$ or PV): The output parameter we want to keep constant (e.g., boiler water level, outlet temperature).
  2. Measuring Element (Sensor + Transducer): Senses the controlled variable and converts it into a standard, clean electrical transmission signal $b(t)$ (typically $4\text{ to }20\text{ mA}$ or $1\text{ to }5\text{ V}$).
  3. Set Point ($r$ or SP): The desired target reference value entered by the operator.
  4. Error Detector: Compares reference $r(t)$ and feedback $b(t)$ to calculate the error: $$ e(t) = r(t) - b(t) $$
  5. Controller: The decision maker. Calculates the required corrective action $m(t)$ based on the magnitude, duration, and rate of error. Standard PID Controller equation: $$ m(t) = K_p\,e(t) + \frac{K_p}{T_i}\int_0^{t} e(\tau)\,d\tau + K_p T_d\,\frac{de(t)}{dt} + m_0 $$ where $K_p$ is proportional gain, $T_i$ is integral (reset) time, $T_d$ is derivative (rate) time, and $m_0$ is controller bias.
  6. Final Control Element: The physical hardware (e.g., pneumatic control valve, electric motor drive, electric heating element) that directly manipulates the flow of material or energy.
  7. Manipulated Variable ($m$): The fluid or energy stream adjusted by the actuator (e.g., steam flow rate, valve opening percentage).
  8. Disturbance (Load): Any uncontrolled external factor that pushes the process variable away from the set point (e.g., sudden change in ambient temperature or feed fluid flow).

1.2 Sensor Time Response – First and Second Order

💡 In Simple Terms: When you plunge a cold thermometer into boiling water, the mercury doesn't instantly jump to $100^\circ\text{C}$. It takes time to absorb heat and reach equilibrium. This delay and dynamic trajectory is the sensor's time response.

1. First-Order Sensor Response

Sensors with a single energy-storage component (like the thermal mass of a bare thermocouple or an electrical RC circuit) obey a first-order differential equation:

$$ \tau\,\frac{db(t)}{dt} + b(t) = K\,c(t) $$

where $\tau$ is the time constant (seconds), $b(t)$ is the sensor output, $c(t)$ is the input, and $K$ is static sensitivity.

(a) Step Input Response: If the input changes abruptly from initial value $b_i$ to final steady value $b_f$ at $t = 0$:

Step-by-step Solution: $$ b(t) = b_f + (b_i - b_f)\,e^{-t/\tau} \quad\iff\quad \frac{b(t) - b_i}{b_f - b_i} = 1 - e^{-t/\tau} $$
  • At $t = 1\tau$: Output reaches $1 - e^{-1} = 63.2\%$ of the total change.
  • At $t = 2\tau$: Output reaches $86.5\%$.
  • At $t = 3\tau$: Output reaches $95.0\%$.
  • At $t = 5\tau$: Output reaches $99.3\%$ (practically settled).

(b) Ramp Input Response: If the input rises steadily at constant rate $R$, $c(t) = R\cdot t$:

$$ b(t) = K R\,(t - \tau) + K R\,\tau\,e^{-t/\tau} $$

After initial transients die away ($e^{-t/\tau} \to 0$), a permanent steady-state dynamic lag error remains:

$$ e_{ss} = K R \tau $$

Takeaway: A smaller time constant $\tau$ means a faster sensor with less lag error.

2. Second-Order Sensor Response

Sensors with two energy storage elements that exchange energy (like mass and spring in an accelerometer or diaphragm and liquid in a pressure sensor) obey:

$$ \frac{1}{\omega_n^2}\,\frac{d^2b(t)}{dt^2} + \frac{2\zeta}{\omega_n}\,\frac{db(t)}{dt} + b(t) = b_f $$

where $\omega_n$ is the undamped natural frequency ($\text{rad/s}$) and $\zeta$ (zeta) is the damping ratio.

The step response depends entirely on $\zeta$:

  • Underdamped ($0 < \zeta < 1$): Fast rise, overshoots the target, and oscillates before settling. $$ b(t) = b_f\left[1 - \frac{e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\,\sin\!\left(\omega_d t + \phi\right)\right] $$ where damped frequency $\omega_d = \omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$ and phase angle $\phi = \cos^{-1}\zeta$.
  • Critically Damped ($\zeta = 1$): Fastest response without any overshoot or oscillation: $$ b(t) = b_f\left[1 - (1 + \omega_n t)\,e^{-\omega_n t}\right] $$
  • Overdamped ($\zeta > 1$): No overshoot, but sluggish and slow to reach final value.

Important Second-Order Metrics:

  • Peak Time ($t_p$): Time taken to reach the first maximum overshoot: $t_p = \dfrac{\pi}{\omega_d}$.
  • Maximum Percentage Overshoot ($M_p$): $$ M_p = \exp\!\left(\frac{-\pi\zeta}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\right)\times 100\% $$
  • Settling Time ($t_s$): Time to stay within $\pm 2\%$ band: $t_s \approx \dfrac{4}{\zeta\omega_n}$.

1.3 Transducer Characteristics and Selection Criteria

💡 In Simple Terms: A sensor feels the physical quantity (e.g., heat), while a transducer converts that energy into an electrical signal (e.g., millivolts). Selecting a transducer is like choosing the right vehicle for a trip: you must check speed, payload, fuel consumption, and ruggedness against road conditions.

Static vs Dynamic Characteristics

CharacteristicTypePlain English Meaning
SensitivityStaticChange in output divided by change in input: $S = \Delta\text{Output} / \Delta\text{Input}$. High sensitivity is desired.
LinearityStaticHow closely the output-vs-input curve resembles a straight line.
HysteresisStaticDifference in sensor reading when approaching the same input from increasing vs decreasing directions.
Repeatability / PrecisionStaticAbility to produce identical readings for the exact same input under identical conditions.
ResolutionStaticThe smallest detectable change in input that produces a noticeable output change.
Span and RangeStaticRange is the operating interval $[X_{min}, X_{max}]$; Span is the difference $(X_{max} - X_{min})$.
Time Constant / Rise TimeDynamicHow quickly the sensor reacts when the input changes suddenly.
Frequency ResponseDynamicThe range of input frequencies over which the transducer maintains accurate amplitude and phase.

Checklist for Selecting an Industrial Transducer

  1. Input Compatibility: Operating principle matches the process variable (temperature, pressure, level, speed).
  2. Range and Span: Covers normal and overload conditions with a safety margin.
  3. Accuracy & Resolution: Meets the precision requirements of the process.
  4. Dynamic Speed: Fast enough time constant so dynamic lag does not destabilise control loops.
  5. Loading Effect: Does not draw noticeable energy or alter the process being measured (e.g., high electrical input impedance, low physical mass).
  6. Environmental Ruggedness: Resists process temperature, humidity, corrosive chemicals, vibration, and EMI noise; explosion-proof if needed.
  7. Standard Output: Compatible with standard industrial inputs ($4\text{ to }20\text{ mA}$, $0\text{ to }10\text{ V}$, RS-485 Modbus).
  8. Reliability, Life & Cost: Long mean-time-between-failures (MTBF), low maintenance, affordable spares.

1.4 Displacement Measurement Using Resistance Potentiometer

💡 In Simple Terms: A potentiometer ("POT") is just a long uniform resistor wire with a slider (wiper) attached to the moving machine part. As the machine moves, the wiper slides along the wire, dividing a fixed voltage. The measured output voltage is directly proportional to position! 
  Excitation Voltage E o----------+
                                  |
                              [ R_p ] Total resistance (length x_t)
                                  |
    Displacement x  ------>  [ Wiper ]=======> Output Voltage e_o (to meter / load R_L)
                                  |
                 0 V o------------+

Ideal Operation (No Load: $R_L \to \infty$)

If the wiper is moved a distance $x$ from the reference end of a track of total length $x_t$:

$$ e_o = E\,\frac{R_x}{R_p} = E\,\frac{x}{x_t} $$

Sensitivity is constant: $S = \dfrac{E}{x_t}\ \text{V/m}$. For rotary pots with total angle $\theta_t$: $e_o = E\dfrac{\theta}{\theta_t}$.

Loading Effect (Real World: Finite Load Resistance $R_L$)

When connected to a display or controller of input resistance $R_L$, current drawn by the load causes the output voltage to droop, introducing non-linearity. Letting normalised displacement $r = x / x_t$:

$$ \frac{e_o}{E} = \frac{r}{1 + \left(\dfrac{R_p}{R_L}\right) r\,(1 - r)} $$

How to eliminate loading error: Ensure $R_L \gg R_p$ (rule of thumb: $R_L \ge 10 R_p$), or insert a high-impedance op-amp voltage follower (buffer) between wiper and load.

Wire-Wound Resolution: For a wire-wound pot of $N$ total turns, the wiper moves in steps of one turn. Hence, resolution is limited to $\dfrac{1}{N}\times 100\%$. Continuous conductive plastic pots give infinite theoretical resolution.

1.5 Capacitive Differential Pressure (DP) Measurement

💡 In Simple Terms: Think of a push-pull see-saw. A flexible metal diaphragm sits right between two fixed metal plates, forming two capacitors. When high pressure pushes from one side, the diaphragm flexes closer to one plate (capacitance goes UP) and farther from the other (capacitance goes DOWN). Measuring the difference between both gives an extremely accurate, linear measurement of pressure difference!

               v                                                   v

               |                                                   |
           (Silicone Oil)                                      (Silicone Oil)
               |                                                   |
               +------------+                                      +------------+
                            v                                      v
                      Fixed Plate P1  === Capacitor C1 ===
                     -------------------------------------
                      SENSING DIAPHRAGM (flexible common plate, moves by distance x)
                     -------------------------------------

                            ^                                      ^
                            |                                      |
         AC excitation E applied across outer plates -> Bridge / Differential Output E_o

Mathematical Derivation of Linearity

With plate area $A$, dielectric permittivity $\varepsilon$, and initial rest gap $d_0$. When pressure difference $\Delta P = P_1 - P_2$ deflects the diaphragm by distance $x$ ($x \propto \Delta P$):

$$ C_1 = \frac{\varepsilon A}{d_0 - x}, \qquad C_2 = \frac{\varepsilon A}{d_0 + x} $$

Applying AC excitation voltage $E$ across plates $P_1$ and $P_2$, the voltage at the centre diaphragm relative to the plates acts as an AC capacitive divider:

$$ E_1 = E\,\frac{C_2}{C_1 + C_2}, \qquad E_2 = E\,\frac{C_1}{C_1 + C_2} $$

The differential output voltage $E_o = E_1 - E_2$ is:

$$ \begin{aligned} E_o &= E\,\frac{C_2 - C_1}{C_1 + C_2} \\ &= E\,\frac{\dfrac{\varepsilon A}{d_0 + x} - \dfrac{\varepsilon A}{d_0 - x}}{\dfrac{\varepsilon A}{d_0 + x} + \dfrac{\varepsilon A}{d_0 - x}} \\ &= E\,\frac{\dfrac{(d_0 - x) - (d_0 + x)}{d_0^2 - x^2}}{\dfrac{(d_0 - x) + (d_0 + x)}{d_0^2 - x^2}} \\ &= E\,\frac{-2x}{2d_0} = -E\,\frac{x}{d_0} \end{aligned} $$
🎯 Major Advantage: Notice that dielectric permittivity $\varepsilon$ and plate area $A$ cancel out completely! This makes the output strictly linear with displacement $x$ (and hence $\Delta P$) and virtually immune to ambient temperature changes in the silicone oil dielectric!

Industrial Uses: (1) Orifice plate flow measurement ($Q \propto \sqrt{\Delta P}$), (2) Liquid level in closed pressurised tanks, (3) Filter clogging monitoring.

1.6 Flow Measurement Using Hot-Wire Anemometer

💡 In Simple Terms: If you blow on a hot spoon of soup, the moving air cools it down. The faster the air moves, the faster heat is carried away. A hot-wire anemometer places an electrically heated microscopic wire into a gas stream; the rate of cooling tells you the exact fluid speed!

Operating Principle & King's Law

A fine tungsten or platinum wire (diameter $\approx 5\,\mu\text{m}$, length $1\text{–}2\text{ mm}$) is placed in the gas stream and heated by an electric current $I$. At thermal equilibrium, electrical heat generated equals heat lost by forced convection:

$$ I^2 R_w = h A\,(T_w - T_f) = (a + b\sqrt{v})\,(T_w - T_f) \qquad \text{(King's Law)} $$

where $R_w$ is wire resistance at temperature $T_w$, $T_f$ is fluid temperature, $v$ is flow velocity, and $a, b$ are empirical calibration constants.

FeatureConstant-Current Mode (CCA)Constant-Temperature Mode (CTA)

1.7 Speed Measurement – Variable Reluctance Tachometer

💡 In Simple Terms: A ferromagnetic toothed wheel spins on a shaft. Next to it sits a magnet wrapped in a coil. Every time a metal tooth flies past the magnet, magnetic reluctance drops and a surge of magnetic flux induces a sharp voltage pulse in the coil. Count the pulses per second, and you instantly know the shaft RPM—without touching the spinning shaft! 
            Permanent Magnet with Pick-up Coil (N turns)
                        +---[ N   S ]---+
                        |   [  Coil ]   |
                        +-------+-------+
                                | Air Gap (varies)
                             _/\_/\_
                           /  Tooth  \  <--- Ferromagnetic Toothed Wheel (Nt teeth)
                          |   Wheel   |      Spins at n RPM
                           \         /

Working Principle & Formula

  1. When a tooth faces the pole, the air gap is small $\implies$ magnetic reluctance $\mathcal{R}$ is minimum $\implies$ magnetic flux $\phi$ is maximum.
  2. When a gap faces the pole, the air gap is large $\implies$ reluctance is maximum $\implies$ flux is minimum.
  3. By Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction, induced EMF is: $$ e(t) = -N\,\frac{d\phi}{dt} = -N\,\frac{d\phi}{d\theta}\cdot\frac{d\theta}{dt} = -N\,\frac{d\phi}{d\theta}\,\omega $$
  4. Each passing tooth generates one complete AC cycle. If the wheel has $N_t$ teeth and turns at $n$ RPM: $$ \text{Pulse Frequency } f = \frac{N_t \cdot n}{60}\ \text{Hz} \quad\implies\quad n = \frac{60\,f}{N_t}\ \text{RPM} $$

Why It's So Accurate: Speed information is carried entirely in the frequency of the pulses, not their voltage amplitude. Even if the sensor vibrates or the air gap fluctuates slightly, frequency is completely unchanged!

Limitation: At very low speeds ($\omega \to 0$), $d\phi/dt$ is tiny and the induced voltage falls below detection threshold. Hence, it cannot measure near-zero speed.

1.8 Phase Measurement – Analog and Digital

💡 In Simple Terms: Phase difference $\phi$ is simply the time delay $\Delta t$ between two sinusoidal waves of the same frequency $f$ crossing zero in the same direction: $\phi = 360^\circ \times (\Delta t / T)$.

1. Analog Method

Both sinusoidal inputs are converted into square waves using Zero-Crossing Detectors (ZCD). The square waves trigger an S-R Flip-Flop (or XOR gate). The flip-flop produces pulses whose width equals time delay $\Delta t$. Passing these pulses through a Low-Pass Filter (LPF) yields a DC voltage directly proportional to phase:

$$ V_{dc} = V_{peak}\cdot\frac{\Delta t}{T} = V_{peak}\cdot\frac{\phi}{360^\circ} \quad\implies\quad \phi = 360^\circ\,\frac{V_{dc}}{V_{peak}} $$

2. Digital Method

The time interval $\Delta t$ enables an AND gate that gates high-frequency clock pulses (frequency $f_c$) into a digital counter. The counter accumulates $N_\phi = f_c \Delta t$ pulses. Over a full period $T$, a second counter accumulates $N_T = f_c T$. The digital display computes:

$$ \phi = 360^\circ\,\frac{N_\phi}{N_T} $$

Digital method provides high resolution, direct numerical readout, and is completely immune to drift and amplitude noise.

Explain the principle of a resistance potentiometer.

3 marks
Apr 2025 · Q2

A resistance potentiometer is a passive displacement transducer that works on the principle of variable electrical resistance via length variation ($R = \rho l / A$). It acts as an adjustable voltage divider.

A fixed DC or AC excitation voltage $E$ is applied across the ends of a uniform resistive element of total length $x_t$. A movable contact (wiper) attached to the moving body picks off voltage $e_o$. For displacement $x$ from the zero end (under open-circuit conditions):

$$ e_o = E\,\frac{x}{x_t} $$

The output voltage is directly proportional to displacement, giving sensitivity $S = E / x_t\ \text{V/m}$.

2. Detailed Description of Each Block

  • Process (Plant): The physical equipment whose variable is being controlled (e.g., steam jacketed kettle, fluid heat exchanger, chemical storage vessel).
  • Controlled Variable ($c$): The specific condition being maintained (e.g., liquid level in metres, steam temperature in $^\circ\text{C}$).
  • Measuring Element (Sensor & Transducer): Measures variable $c(t)$ and produces clean feedback signal $b(t)$, scaled to standard $4\text{–}20\text{ mA}$ range.
  • Error Detector: Computes algebraic difference: $e(t) = r(t) - b(t)$. An error exists whenever target differs from reality.
  • Controller: Evaluates error $e(t)$ and generates control output $m(t)$ using PID algorithm to eliminate the deviation quickly and stably.
  • Final Control Element (Actuator & Valve): The muscle of the system. Converts controller electric signal ($4\text{–}20\text{ mA}$) into mechanical travel to throttle fluid flow.
  • Manipulated Variable: The flow of mass or energy adjusted by the valve (e.g., heating steam flow).
  • Disturbance (Load): External upsets (e.g., sudden increase in cold liquid inflow) that cause $c(t)$ to drift.

3. Industrial Example: Boiler Drum Water Level Control

  • Objective: Maintain drum water level at $H = 2.0\text{ m}$.
  • Sensor / Transducer: A Capacitive Differential Pressure Transmitter measures pressure head at the drum base and outputs $4\text{ to }20\text{ mA}$.
  • Controller: A digital PID controller compares measured level with $2.0\text{ m}$.
  • Control Action: If steam demand spikes, water level drops to $1.8\text{ m}$. Error $e = 2.0 - 1.8 = +0.2\text{ m}$. The controller increases its output to open the feedwater control valve. Feedwater flow increases until level returns exactly to $2.0\text{ m}$.

Q. Explain first-order and second-order time responses of a sensor with relevant sketches and output equations.

7 – 8 marks
Jun 2023 · Q11(a)Apr 2025 · Q11(b)Model Paper Q11(b)

1. First-Order Sensor Time Response

Described by differential equation: $\tau\,\dfrac{db(t)}{dt} + b(t) = b_f$.

Derivation for Step Input ($b(0) = b_i$ to $b_f$):
Taking Laplace Transform: $\tau\,[s B(s) - b_i] + B(s) = \dfrac{b_f}{s}$. Rearranging:
$$ B(s) = \frac{b_f}{s\,(\tau s + 1)} + \frac{\tau b_i}{\tau s + 1} $$ Taking Inverse Laplace Transform yields the famous exponential response: $$ b(t) = b_f + (b_i - b_f)\,e^{-t/\tau} \quad\iff\quad \frac{b(t) - b_i}{b_f - b_i} = 1 - e^{-t/\tau} $$

Key Landmarks: $63.2\%$ at $1\tau$, $86.5\%$ at $2\tau$, $95.0\%$ at $3\tau$, and $99.3\%$ at $5\tau$. Rise time ($10\%\text{ to }90\%$) is $t_r = 2.2\tau$.

2. Second-Order Sensor Time Response

Described by: $\dfrac{1}{\omega_n^2}\,\dfrac{d^2b}{dt^2} + \dfrac{2\zeta}{\omega_n}\,\dfrac{db}{dt} + b = b_f$.

Underdamped Solution ($0 < \zeta < 1$):
Roots of characteristic equation are complex conjugates: $s = -\zeta\omega_n \pm j\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$. The normalized step response is: $$ y(t) = 1 - \frac{e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\,\sin\!\left(\omega_d t + \phi\right) $$ where damped natural frequency $\omega_d = \omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$ and phase angle $\phi = \cos^{-1}\zeta$. 
 Output
  1.4 |      Underdamped (zeta = 0.2) -> prominent overshoot Mp
  1.2 |       ,-.
  1.0 |-----+--\-'------------------------ Steady-state final value bf
      |    /    '--._  Critically Damped (zeta = 1) -> fastest monotonic rise
  0.5 |   /          '------------------ Overdamped (zeta > 1) -> slow
  0.0 |/'_________________________________ Time t

Three Performance Metrics:

  1. Peak Time ($t_p$): $t_p = \dfrac{\pi}{\omega_d} = \dfrac{\pi}{\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$
  2. Peak Overshoot ($M_p$): $M_p = \exp\!\left(\dfrac{-\pi\zeta}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\right)\times 100\%$
  3. Settling Time ($t_s$): $t_s \approx \dfrac{4}{\zeta\omega_n}$ (for $2\%$ tolerance band).

Q. Explain the criteria for the selection of a transducer for an industrial application. Mention applications.

Selecting an industrial transducer requires balancing 8 engineering criteria:

  1. Measurement Principle & Compatibility: Must naturally suit the physical variable (e.g., RTD/Thermocouple for temperature, strain gauge for force, LVDT for displacement).
  2. Operating Range & Overload Capacity: Must span full operating values plus a minimum $25\text{–}50\%$ safety margin for process surges.
  3. Accuracy, Linearity & Resolution: Must meet the tolerance budget of the process without expensive software linearisation.
  4. Dynamic Speed & Bandwidth: Time constant $\tau$ or bandwidth must exceed the rate of change of the measured variable to avoid dynamic lag.
  5. Loading Effect: Must have high input impedance (or low mass) so it does not draw significant energy or distort the process.
  6. Environmental Ruggedness: Must handle corrosive fluids, vibration, ambient extremes ($-20^\circ\text{C to }+85^\circ\text{C}$), dust (IP67), and hazardous explosive atmospheres (ATEX / Intrinsic Safety).
  7. Electrical Interface: Standard industrial output ($4\text{–}20\text{ mA}$ 2-wire current loop, $0\text{–}10\text{ V}$, or digital RS-485 Modbus).
  8. Economics & Maintainability: Purchase cost, availability of spares, ease of calibration, and long MTBF.

Industrial Applications:

  • Strain Gauge Load Cell: Weighing hoppers and conveyor batching in cement plants.
  • Capacitive DP Transmitter: Steam flow metering via orifice plate in power plants.
  • Pt100 RTD: Temperature control in pharmaceutical bioreactors.
  • Variable Reluctance Tachometer: Turbine shaft speed monitoring in hydro stations.
  • Piezoelectric Accelerometer: Bearing vibration diagnostics on industrial pumps.

Q. Illustrate capacitive differential pressure measurement with complete derivation.

7 marks
Jun 2023 · Q12(a)May 2024 · Q11(b)Apr 2026 · Q12(a)

1. Construction & Principle

The sensor features a taut metallic sensing diaphragm enclosed between two fixed perforated metal plates ($P_1$ and $P_2$), forming two capacitors $C_1$ and $C_2$. The capsule is filled with incompressible silicone oil and isolated from corrosive process fluid by flexible isolating diaphragms.

         High Pressure P1                                    Low Pressure P2
                |                                                   |
                v                                                   v
         [ Isolating Diaphragm ]                             [ Isolating Diaphragm ]
                |                                                   |
            (Silicone Oil)                                      (Silicone Oil)
                |                                                   |
                +-------------> Fixed Plate P1  === C1 ===
                                --------------------------
                                SENSING DIAPHRAGM (moves x)
                                --------------------------
                +-------------> Fixed Plate P2  === C2 ===

2. Mathematical Derivation of Linear Output

When $P_1 > P_2$, the pressure difference $\Delta P = P_1 - P_2$ causes the diaphragm to deflect towards $P_2$ by distance $x$ ($x = \dfrac{A_d}{k}\,\Delta P$). Capacitances become:

$$ C_1 = \frac{\varepsilon A}{d_0 - x}, \qquad C_2 = \frac{\varepsilon A}{d_0 + x} $$

Applying AC voltage $E$ across fixed plates, the voltages across $C_1$ and $C_2$ are:

$$ E_1 = E\,\frac{C_2}{C_1 + C_2}, \qquad E_2 = E\,\frac{C_1}{C_1 + C_2} $$

Taking the differential output $E_o = E_1 - E_2$:

$$ \begin{aligned} E_o &= E\,\frac{C_2 - C_1}{C_1 + C_2} \\ &= E\,\frac{\dfrac{\varepsilon A}{d_0 + x} - \dfrac{\varepsilon A}{d_0 - x}}{\dfrac{\varepsilon A}{d_0 + x} + \dfrac{\varepsilon A}{d_0 - x}} \\ &= E\,\frac{\dfrac{(d_0 - x) - (d_0 + x)}{d_0^2 - x^2}}{\dfrac{(d_0 - x) + (d_0 + x)}{d_0^2 - x^2}} \\ &= E\,\frac{-2x}{2d_0} = -E\,\frac{x}{d_0} \end{aligned} $$

Substituting diaphragm deflection $x = \dfrac{A_d}{k}\Delta P$:

$$ |E_o| = E\left(\frac{A_d}{k\,d_0}\right)\Delta P $$

Conclusion: Output voltage is strictly proportional to differential pressure $\Delta P$. Notice $\varepsilon$ and $A$ cancel out, eliminating errors from dielectric temperature variations.

Q. Explain displacement measurement using resistance potentiometer and derive its loading effect.

7 marks
Jun 2023 · Q12(b)Apr 2026 · Q12(b)

1. Construction & Principle

A resistance potentiometer converts mechanical stroke into a variable voltage. It consists of a precision resistive track (wire-wound, conductive plastic, or cermet) of total resistance $R_p$ and total length $x_t$, excited by voltage $E$. A sliding wiper mechanically coupled to the moving object picks off voltage $e_o$.

2. Ideal (Unloaded) Condition

$$ e_o = E\,\frac{R_x}{R_p} = E\,\frac{x}{x_t} = E\cdot r \qquad \left(r = \frac{x}{x_t}\right) $$

3. Derivation of Loading Error with Finite Load $R_L$

When connected to a meter or controller of load resistance $R_L$, the parallel combination of the tapped section $R_x = r R_p$ and $R_L$ is:

$$ R_{parallel} = \frac{R_x \cdot R_L}{R_x + R_L} = \frac{r R_p R_L}{r R_p + R_L} $$

Total resistance seen by source $E$ is $(1 - r)R_p + R_{parallel}$. Output voltage across load is:

$$ e_o = E\,\frac{R_{parallel}}{(1 - r)R_p + R_{parallel}} $$

Substituting $R_{parallel}$ and dividing numerator and denominator by $R_L$:

$$ \frac{e_o}{E} = \frac{r}{1 + \left(\dfrac{R_p}{R_L}\right) r\,(1 - r)} $$

Analysis: The term $\left(\dfrac{R_p}{R_L}\right)r(1-r)$ represents non-linear loading error. Maximum error occurs near $r \approx 0.67$. To keep loading error under $1\%$, select $R_L \ge 10 R_p$ or use an op-amp buffer.

Q. Explain the principle and operation of variable reluctance tachometer.

7 marks
Apr 2025 · Q12(b)Model Paper Q12(a)

Principle: Non-contact rotational speed measurement using electromagnetic induction caused by periodic variation in magnetic reluctance.

         Pick-up Coil (N turns) wound on Permanent Magnet
                      +---[ N   S ]---+
                      |   [ Coil  ]   | ===> Induced AC Pulses e(t)
                      +-------+-------+
                              | Variable Air Gap
                           _/\_/\_
                         /  Tooth  \  Toothed Ferromagnetic Rotor (Nt teeth)
                        |   Wheel   | Attached to rotating shaft (n RPM)
                         \         /

Working & Mathematical Formula

  1. As the shaft rotates, ferromagnetic teeth and air gaps alternately pass the pole piece.
  2. Tooth alignment $\implies$ small air gap $\implies$ minimum reluctance $\implies$ maximum magnetic flux $\phi$.
  3. Gap alignment $\implies$ large air gap $\implies$ maximum reluctance $\implies$ minimum flux $\phi$.
  4. By Faraday's Law, induced voltage in coil is: $$ e(t) = -N\,\frac{d\phi}{dt} = -N\,\frac{d\phi}{d\theta}\,\omega $$
  5. Each tooth generates 1 electrical cycle. For $N_t$ teeth and speed $n$ RPM, the output frequency is: $$ f = \frac{N_t \cdot n}{60}\ \text{Hz} \quad\implies\quad n = \frac{60\,f}{N_t}\ \text{RPM} $$

Merits: Robust, non-contact, requires no external excitation, speed output is in immune frequency format. Limitation: Output amplitude drops to zero at near-zero speed.

Q. Explain analog and digital phase measurement techniques with block diagrams.

7 marks
May 2024 · Q12(a)

Phase measurement determines angle $\phi$ between two sinusoidal signals $v_1(t) = V_m\sin(\omega t)$ and $v_2(t) = V_m\sin(\omega t - \phi)$.

1. Analog Method

 v1(t) --->[ Zero Crossing Detector 1 ]---> Set (S)   +-------+
                                                      |  S-R  |---> Pulse Train --->[ Low-Pass Filter ]---> DC Voltage V_dc
 v2(t) --->[ Zero Crossing Detector 2 ]---> Reset (R) |  FF   |     (Width dt)      (Averaging)             (V_dc ~ phi)
                                                      +-------+

Operation: Both signals are converted to square waves by comparators. Positive edge of $v_1$ sets an S-R flip-flop; positive edge of $v_2$ resets it. The flip-flop produces pulses of width $\Delta t = \dfrac{\phi}{360^\circ} T$. A low-pass filter extracts average DC voltage:

$$ V_{dc} = V_{peak}\cdot\frac{\Delta t}{T} = V_{peak}\cdot\frac{\phi}{360^\circ} \implies \phi = 360^\circ\,\frac{V_{dc}}{V_{peak}} $$

2. Digital Method

 Flip-Flop Gate Pulse (Width dt) -------->| AND |----->[ Counter 1 ]-----> Count N_phi
 High-Freq Clock fc (e.g. 1 MHz) -------->|     |
 Period Gate (Full Period T) ------------>| AND |----->[ Counter 2 ]-----> Count N_T

Operation: The gating pulse $\Delta t$ gates a high-frequency crystal clock ($f_c$) into counter 1 ($N_\phi = f_c\Delta t$). Over a full period $T$, counter 2 counts $N_T = f_c T$. The digital processor computes:

$$ \phi = 360^\circ\,\frac{N_\phi}{N_T} $$

Completely eliminates analog component drift and meter calibration errors.

Q. What is a hot-wire anemometer? Explain constant-current type and mention its advantage.

7 marks
May 2024 · Q12(b)

Hot-Wire Anemometer: A thermal transducer for measuring fluid flow velocity. A fine electrically heated platinum or tungsten wire ($5\,\mu\text{m}$ diameter) placed in the stream loses heat by forced convection.

King's Law: Heat dissipated equals heat carried away: $I^2 R_w = (a + b\sqrt{v})(T_w - T_f)$.

Constant-Current Working

 Constant Current Source Is
           +---------------+
           |               |
         [ R1 ]         [ Hot Wire Rw in fluid stream ]  ====> Bridge Output Voltage e_o
           |               |
         [ R2 ]         [ R3 ]
           +---------------+
  1. The wire forms one arm of a Wheatstone bridge driven by a constant current source $I_s$.
  2. When fluid velocity $v$ increases, convective cooling lowers wire temperature $T_w$.
  3. Because platinum has positive temperature coefficient ($R_w = R_0[1+\alpha(T_w - T_0)]$), lower temperature lowers $R_w$.
  4. This unbalances the bridge, producing output voltage $e_o$ that correlates with $\sqrt{v}$.

Major Advantage: Circuit is simple, inexpensive, and exhibits very low electrical noise. (Limitation: Slow thermal response; prone to wire burnout if flow suddenly stops).
